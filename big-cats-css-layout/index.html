What counts as a big cat

The narrow answer is the genus Panthera, named and described by Lorenz Oken in 1816, with the leopard as its type species. Five species are alive today: the lion, the tiger, the jaguar, the leopard and the snow leopard. The snow leopard was filed separately as Uncia uncia for most of the twentieth century, and only in 2008 did the IUCN assessors accept its place in Panthera.

In ordinary use the phrase stretches further. The cheetah and the cougar get called big cats too, and neither belongs to Panthera. No size threshold decides the matter, which makes "big cat" a term about how people talk rather than a rank in taxonomy.

Why only four of them roar

All five species share an incompletely ossified hyoid bone and a larynx adapted with large vocal folds covered by a pad of fibro-elastic tissue. That combination is what makes a roar possible, and it is the main thing separating this genus from every other cat.

The snow leopard is the exception. Its vocal folds measure 9 mm, short enough that they put up less resistance to moving air, so the sound never builds into a roar. At the other end of the genus sits the lion, whose larynx is the longest of the five. In the right conditions a lion's roar carries 8 or even 10 km.

All five are on the Red List

Not one of the five is listed as secure. The lion has been Vulnerable since 1996 and the jaguar Near Threatened since 2002. The tiger is Endangered. The leopard and the snow leopard are both Vulnerable, the latter because fewer than 10,000 mature individuals are estimated to remain in the wild.
